Does Watching Someone's Lips Help a Model Understand an Accent? Mostly Not — Yet.

A research note on audio-visual speech recognition for non-standard English. This work is under peer review, so we've kept authorship and dataset details anonymous for now.

The intuition we started with

When you talk to someone across a noisy room, you lean on their lips. Speech perception is multimodal — the audio and the visual signal fuse into one percept, so tightly that a mismatched video can make you hear a different sound entirely (the McGurk effect). Audio-visual speech recognition (AVSR) leans on the same idea: give a model the audio and a cropped video of the speaker's mouth, and the lip movements should disambiguate what the ears alone can't resolve.

That trick is well established for one specific problem: acoustic noise. When the audio is degraded, the video helps.

Our question was different. What about linguistic variation — regional dialects and non-native accents — rather than acoustic noise? An accent isn't noise; it's a systematic shift in how sounds are produced. If lips help humans adapt to an unfamiliar accent (and the psycholinguistics says they do), maybe they'd help models too. And this matters, because ASR systems are notoriously worse for speakers outside Mainstream American English.

So we built a dataset and tested it. The short version: the visual modality did not rescue accented speech, and test-time adaptation helped only modestly. Below is what we found and why we think it happened.

The dataset gap that forced us to build our own

You can't study AVSR on non-standard English if the data doesn't exist — and it mostly doesn't. Existing audio-visual corpora skew heavily toward native, standard speakers, or they cover accents but withhold the audio, or they include only a handful of samples. There was no benchmark that paired audio + video + dialect/accent labels at any usable scale.

We assembled one: 2.07 hours of read speech, 54 videos, 49 distinct speakers, 1,051 utterances, spanning both regional dialects (American regional varieties, Canadian, British, Australian, Irish, Indian, Jamaican, New Zealand) and non-native accents (L1s including Mandarin, Cantonese, Vietnamese, Malay, Polish, Russian, Italian, Spanish, French, German, Persian, Urdu). Roughly 40% of utterances are non-native/accented, 60% dialectal.

We deliberately used read speech — everyone reads the same two standard passages (The Rainbow Passage and Comma Gets a Cure, both engineered to contain every phoneme of American English). That controls lexical content, so any variation you see is sociophonetic — how the sounds are made — not differences in word choice. We used one passage as a development set for tuning and held the other out as the test set.

Processing pipeline (the part that's fiddly)

  • Stream separation with ffmpeg, audio resampled to 16 kHz.
  • Lip-region crops at 96×96, done frame-by-frame: detect facial landmarks, temporally smooth them, align to a mean face via a similarity transform. Static cropping breaks under head motion; this dynamic approach holds alignment.
  • Sentence segmentation via CTC forced alignment against the known passage transcripts, using an encoder-only speech foundation model. CTC gives a monotonic frame-to-token mapping, which converts cleanly into timestamps.
  • Manual cleanup: raw CTC boundaries occasionally clipped words, so we padded segment edges (~0.2 s) and hand-corrected any utterance with WER above 10% or boundary bleed.

Finding 1: Adding video didn't help — it slightly hurt

We evaluated two model families across three input conditions — ASR (audio only), VSR (video only), and AVSR (both):

  • a generalist multimodal model (unified across text/image/audio/video, not specialized for AVSR), and
  • a specialist AVSR model (a Whisper audio backbone with lip features injected through gated cross-attention).

The headline, which surprised us: AVSR was worse than ASR for both models. Adding the video, on average, did not improve transcription of non-standard English — and often nudged it the wrong way. The generalist went from single-digit ASR WER to a higher AVSR WER; the specialist showed the same direction.

Why would a second source of information make things worse? A few explanations, not mutually exclusive:

  1. Cross-modal integration is itself hard. Fusing two streams is only a win if the fusion is well-calibrated. Under distribution shift (an accent the model hasn't seen much of), the model may not know how much to trust the lips, and mis-weighted fusion can inject noise rather than remove it.
  2. The lips themselves may be misleading. Non-native speakers often produce sounds with atypical articulatory configurations. The visual signal for an accented /th/ might not match the model's learned template, so it actively pulls the prediction off.
  3. Visual gains may be conditional on audio difficulty. In the noise literature, video helps most when the audio is bad. Clean-but-accented audio may not cross whatever difficulty threshold makes the visual channel pay off.

The VSR result makes the diagnosis concrete

Video-only recognition collapsed. The generalist essentially failed entirely — for almost every video-only clip it stopped transcribing and fell back to generic filler text like "I'm not sure," a tell that an instruction-tuned model defaults to its text priors when the input carries no signal it can use. The lip-reading specialist did better than that but still landed at very high WER.

The takeaway: these models barely read lips at all. The audio encoder is doing essentially all the work, and the visual pathway contributes little. That reframes the AVSR-worse-than-ASR result — it's not that the video is misused so much as that the model was never really using it, and forcing it into the mix mostly adds instability.

Finding 2: Test-time adaptation helps a little, and the method matters more than its presence

If the model is struggling under distribution shift, one lightweight fix is test-time adaptation (TTA): at inference time, let the model update a small subset of its own parameters using a self-supervised objective — no labels, just the model's own confidence. The standard objective is entropy minimization: nudge the model to be more confident in its own predictions on the unfamiliar input. We reset the model after each speaker's passage, which mirrors how a human adapts to an accent after a short exposure and then "forgets" it for the next talker.

We compared four adaptation variants on the accented subset of the test set. Here's what actually moved (WER, lower is better; baseline with no adaptation is 11.07%):

Three things worth pulling out:

  • The best objective gave a real, ~1.9-point absolute WER reduction. Modest, but genuine, and in the right direction.
  • Continual adaptation was basically neutral — carrying the adapted weights across utterances didn't accumulate into a win here.
  • Pseudo-labeling actively hurt. Training on the model's own hard decodings of accented speech reinforces its mistakes — a self-fulfilling error loop. This is the cleanest signal in the table: the wrong self-supervised objective doesn't just fail to help, it degrades.

The audio-only (ASR) runs pointed the same way — the reward-based and continual variants produced the larger reductions — though we're still tightening those experiments (more on that below).

And VSR? Adaptation couldn't save it. Video-only WER stayed near-random after TTA. That's expected: entropy minimization sharpens a signal the model can already partly follow. If there's no usable visual signal to begin with, there's no gradient direction worth descending.

What we think it all means

The clean narrative "add lips, get robustness" holds for acoustic noise but did not transfer to linguistic variation in our experiments. The bottleneck isn't a lack of visual data at inference — it's that current models don't meaningfully integrate the visual modality for speech that's phonetically out of distribution. You can't adapt your way out of a modality you were never really using.

TTA is a useful lever, but a small one, and only with the right objective. The gap between entropy minimization (helps) and pseudo-labeling (hurts) says the interesting research question isn't whether to adapt but what to adapt on.

What we're doing next

This is an honest work-in-progress, and a few threads are still open:

  • Firming up the ASR baselines. Our accented-subset ASR runs surfaced some run-to-run variance in the no-adaptation baseline — a known headache with modern inference stacks (floating-point non-associativity, batch-size-dependent kernels). We're pinning these down against a single frozen baseline before we make strong ASR claims.
  • Filling in the adaptation grid. Several variant/condition cells (especially video-only) are still running.
  • Making models actually look at the lips. The deeper problem is visual under-utilization. We're probing where in the network accent information concentrates, and whether targeted interventions can push the model to rely on the visual channel when the audio is ambiguous — rather than defaulting to audio and treating the video as decoration.
  • Selective fusion. Instead of always fusing both modalities, use the video only when the audio-only prediction is uncertain — turning AVSR into a fallback rather than a default, and sidestepping the integration cost when it isn't needed.

If there's one line to take away: multimodality is not automatically robustness. A second input helps only when the model knows how to weigh it — and for accented and dialectal speech, today's models mostly don't. That's the gap our dataset and our follow-up work are built to close.
